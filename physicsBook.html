Book Design — Archival Work

in theory + in practice: my relationship with physics

A personal archive book that weaves together formal physics concepts with handwritten notes, diagrams, and the lived experience of learning a discipline that resists easy understanding.

Year 2025
Role Book Design, Editorial Design, Writing
Tools InDesign, Illustrator, Photography
Type Book Design — Personal Archive

Physics has a gap in it — between the equations on the board and what they actually mean for how the world works. This book lives in that gap. It's part textbook, part journal, part design object: a document of what it feels like to move through a discipline that demands both rigorous logic and genuine intuition.

The structure mirrors the duality in the title. Each chapter opens with formal theory — a concept, a law, a derivation — rendered with the clarity of a well-designed textbook. The facing page is its counterpart: personal, annotated, sometimes messy. Real notes from real problem sets. Margin scrawls. Moments of confusion and breakthrough, preserved exactly as they happened.

"Physics isn't just something you learn. It's something you argue with, fail at, and eventually, if you're lucky, start to trust."

Design Approach

The editorial design system holds the tension between formal and personal deliberately. Clean grid, precise typesetting, and high-contrast diagrams carry the theoretical sections. Handwritten elements, scanned pages, and intentionally unresolved layouts carry the experiential ones. Neither side overrides the other — the book asks you to hold both at once.

Typography leans on a classical serif for body text, nodding to the long tradition of scientific publishing, while monospace handles all mathematical notation and labels. The result feels authoritative and intimate in equal measure.

Personal Archive

The source material is entirely personal — notebooks, problem sets, lab reports, and photos spanning multiple years of study. Organizing and sequencing these required thinking about narrative as much as design: what does it mean to show someone else your confusion? What does it mean to frame struggle as content worth preserving?

The answer this book arrives at is that the struggle is the most honest part — and the most worth keeping.
